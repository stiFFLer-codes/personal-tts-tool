### PART 4
Title: The natural ice trade
Voices: Narrator=bm_george, Lecturer=bf_alice
Narrator: Part 4. You will hear a lecturer giving a talk about the history of the international trade in natural ice. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40.
[Pause 45]
Narrator: Now listen carefully and answer questions 31 to 40.
Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. In this series of lectures on the history of trade, we've mostly looked at goods like spices, tea and cotton. Today I want to talk about a product that seems, at first, rather unlikely: ice. For most of the nineteenth century, natural ice, cut from frozen lakes and rivers, was shipped all over the world, and it changed the way millions of people ate and drank.
Lecturer: Before the nineteenth century, ice was something of a luxury. Wealthy families in Europe and North America often had ice houses on their estates, where ice collected in winter could be stored under the ground, sometimes for months. But ordinary people rarely saw ice in summer, and in hot countries it was almost unknown.
Lecturer: The man who changed this was Frederic Tudor, a young businessman from Boston. In the early eighteen hundreds, Tudor had the idea that ice from the frozen ponds of New England could be sold in the Caribbean, where the heat was intense all year round. Most people thought he was mad. In fact, when he tried to find a ship to carry his first cargo, no shipowner in Boston would agree. They were afraid that, as the ice melted, the water would fill the hold and the ship would sink. In the end, Tudor had to buy his own ship.
Lecturer: That first voyage, in eighteen oh six, took his ice to the island of Martinique. Surprisingly, a good deal of the ice actually survived the journey. The real problem was what happened when it arrived. The local people had never used ice before, and more importantly, there was nowhere to store it, so much of it simply melted on the dock. Tudor lost a great deal of money, and over the next few years he was even sent to prison for debt. But he didn't give up.
Lecturer: Over the following decades, Tudor and others solved the practical problems one by one. The first was insulation. Tudor experimented with all sorts of materials to pack around the ice, including straw, hay and even rice husks. The most effective, and also the cheapest, turned out to be sawdust. The timber mills of Maine produced huge quantities of it as waste, so it cost almost nothing, and it protected the ice far better than anything else.
Lecturer: The second problem was that ice was cut from the ponds by hand, using saws and axes, which was slow and produced blocks of all different shapes and sizes. In the eighteen twenties, one of Tudor's suppliers, a man called Nathaniel Wyeth, invented a plough, pulled by horses, which cut deep lines across the surface of the frozen pond in a grid pattern. The ice could then be broken into blocks that were all exactly the same size. This mattered more than you might think. Because the blocks were regular, they could be stacked tightly together, with very little air between them, and that meant much less of the ice melted during the voyage.
Lecturer: With these improvements, the trade grew rapidly. In eighteen thirty-three, a ship called the Tuscany carried around a hundred and eighty tonnes of ice from Boston all the way to Calcutta in India, a journey of four months that crossed the equator twice. Just over half of the cargo arrived, which was considered a great success. The British community there used the ice to cool their drinks, of course, but it was also put to medical use. Doctors used it in hospitals to bring down the temperature of patients suffering from fever, and it was said to have saved many lives.
Lecturer: Ice from New England became famous across the world, and one brand in particular, Wenham Lake ice, became so fashionable in London that it was even supplied to the royal family. This led to a rather amusing episode. Norway, which also had plenty of frozen lakes, began exporting ice to Britain, and one Norwegian company went so far as to give one of its lakes a new name. They called it Wenham Lake, so that they could sell their ice under the famous name.
Lecturer: The availability of cheap ice had an enormous effect on daily life. In American cities, ice was delivered to homes every day by horse and cart, and many families bought an ice box, a wooden cupboard lined with metal, which kept food fresh for much longer. The ice trade also transformed the food industry. Meat could now be transported long distances by rail in wagons packed with ice. And there was a significant effect on what people drank. Lager, which needs to be kept cold while it is being made, had traditionally only been brewed in winter. With a reliable supply of ice, breweries could produce it throughout the summer, and its popularity grew enormously.
Lecturer: So why did the trade disappear? There were several reasons. As cities and factories expanded in the second half of the century, many of the rivers and lakes where ice was harvested became seriously polluted. Health officials discovered that ice taken from some rivers was contaminated with sewage, and newspapers warned their readers that it could spread disease. On top of this, a series of unusually warm winters in the eighteen eighties and nineties meant there were years when there was hardly any ice to harvest at all. Meanwhile, machines that could produce ice artificially were becoming cheaper and more reliable, and by the nineteen twenties the natural ice trade had almost completely disappeared.
Lecturer: Today, there are few physical reminders of this once enormous industry. Some of the old ice houses still survive, particularly in Britain, and interestingly, conservationists have found that their cool, dark and stable conditions make them ideal places for bats to spend the winter. Several have now been protected for exactly that reason.
Lecturer: Next week, we'll be looking at how mechanical refrigeration developed, and at its effect on international trade.
