Title: Part 4 – The sea otter: decline and recovery
Narrator: Now turn to Part 4.
Narrator: Part 4. You will hear a lecturer on an ecology course giving a talk about the decline and recovery of the sea otter.
Narrator: First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40.
[Pause 45]
Narrator: Now listen carefully and answer questions 31 to 40.
Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. Today we're continuing our series on marine conservation by looking at one of its genuine success stories, the sea otter. I'm going to begin with the animal's biology and some of its remarkable adaptations. Then I'll look at how hunting brought it to the edge of extinction, before turning to what its disappearance, and later its return, taught ecologists about how coastal ecosystems function. Finally, I'll consider the threats it still faces.
Lecturer: Let's start with the biology. Sea otters live along the coasts of the North Pacific, from Japan and Russia round to Alaska and California, and they're the smallest of all marine mammals. Now, you might expect them to keep warm in cold water the same way that seals and whales do, with a thick layer of blubber beneath the skin. In fact, they have almost no fat of this kind. Instead, they depend on their fur, which is the densest of any animal, with up to a million hairs in a single square inch. Air trapped among those hairs forms an insulating layer, which is why otters spend several hours every day grooming.
Lecturer: Sea otters are also one of the few mammals apart from primates that use tools. An otter will dive to the sea floor, collect a clam or a mussel, and tuck it, together with a suitable stone, into a loose pouch of skin under its foreleg. Back at the surface, it floats on its back, rests the stone on its chest, and bangs the shellfish against it until the shell cracks open.
Lecturer: All of this activity demands a great deal of energy. Because they have so little insulating fat, otters must maintain an extremely high metabolism to generate body heat, and as a result an adult eats roughly a quarter of its own body weight every single day.
Lecturer: Turning now to the decline of the species. Its luxurious fur was, of course, also its downfall. From the middle of the seventeen hundreds, Russian expeditions began hunting sea otters in large numbers, and they were later joined by British and American traders. Although much of the hunting was organised from Russia, the most lucrative market for the pelts was actually China, where they were made into robes for the wealthy and fetched extraordinary prices.
Lecturer: By the early twentieth century, a global population that may once have numbered several hundred thousand had fallen to perhaps two thousand animals, scattered in small, isolated groups. Individual countries had passed laws to protect them, but these were largely ignored. What finally brought the commercial hunt to an end was a treaty, signed in nineteen eleven by four nations, which protected both otters and fur seals.
Lecturer: Another key topic is what the otter's disappearance revealed about ecosystems. One of the otter's favourite foods is the sea urchin, a spiny creature that grazes on kelp, the giant seaweed that forms underwater forests. Once the otters were gone, the urchins multiplied rapidly and stripped the kelp away, leaving bare, rocky sea floor. This led researchers to describe the otter as a keystone species, meaning one whose influence on its ecosystem is far greater than its numbers would suggest.
Lecturer: The consequences went well beyond the kelp itself. Kelp forests support a huge variety of marine life. Seals and seabirds hunt there, but their most important role is as a nursery: young fish hide among the fronds, safe from predators, until they're large enough to survive in open water. Where the kelp disappeared, much of this life disappeared with it.
Lecturer: Finally, let's turn to recovery and the threats that remain. After nineteen eleven, the surviving groups slowly began to grow, and from the nineteen sixties, wildlife officials helped the process along by moving otters to coastlines where they had died out. Interestingly, the first of these relocations was prompted not by conservation at all, but by plans for underground nuclear tests on an Alaskan island, which threatened the otters living there.
Lecturer: Today there are well over a hundred thousand sea otters, but their recovery has been uneven. Oil remains a serious danger, because even a small amount destroys the insulating quality of their fur. In California, scientists were puzzled by a high death rate until they discovered that many otters were dying from a parasite that originates in domestic cats. It's thought to be washed into the sea in waste water from towns along the coast.
Lecturer: There's also a human conflict. As otters return to areas where they've been absent for a century, they compete directly with people who make a living from the sea. Fishermen who catch salmon are largely unaffected, but the shellfish industry, which depends on crabs, clams and abalone, has seen its catches fall sharply, and some divers have called for otter numbers to be controlled.
Lecturer: So, to conclude, the sea otter shows us both how quickly a species can be pushed to the brink and how a single predator can shape an entire ecosystem. Its recovery is also a reminder that conservation success often raises new questions, in this case about how we share coastal resources, which we are only beginning to answer.
Narrator: That is the end of Part 4.
Narrator: That is the end of the Listening test.
